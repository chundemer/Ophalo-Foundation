Setting up your Price Book

The Price Book is your business's private library of the parts, equipment, services, and fees you use again and again — each with an internal cost and a standard sell price. This is how Owners and Admins build and maintain it.

For: Owners & Admins Field users: just pick items — no pricing shown Draft v1

One private source of truth. Add your common items once; record what each costs you and what you charge; group repeatable jobs into assemblies; and add a few nudges to help the team remember related work.

It is internal. Customers never see internal costs, margins, categories, aliases, common-item flags, or nudge rules. Field staff can pick approved items while documenting work without seeing any of the money side.

Start small. A clean catalog of your top 20–50 items beats a big one full of duplicates and blanks. There is no spreadsheet import — you build it deliberately, in OpHalo.

Where it lives

Price Book in the top navigation. Three tabs.

01Catalog items

One reusable thing your business sells, uses, or records.

Every catalog item has a type:

Material

A part or consumable — filter, capacitor, pipe.

Equipment

A durable installed item — condenser, furnace, water heater.

Service

Labor or a defined service — diagnostic visit, maintenance, install labor.

Fee

A charge — service call, permit, disposal.

The list shows the name, SKU, type, unit of measure, and both money figures side by side. A missing cost shows as a dash so it's easy to spot items that still need financial setup. An item that's only ever part of a bundle can show "No standalone price."

02Anatomy of an item

Open any item to see its economics, aliases, and metadata.

The item page shows sell price, direct cost, gross profit, margin %, and markup % once both numbers are set — the office's quick read on whether a common job is priced sustainably. Two buttons up top: Update pricing & cost and Edit item details (name, SKU, category, common flag).

03Cost and sell price

Expected cost stays internal. It is never customer-facing.

Internal cost is your expected direct cost — a standard, not a promise that every job buys at that price. Sell price is what you intend to charge. Update the cost whenever a supplier price changes; the update applies to future use, and work already recorded keeps the price/cost that applied at the time.

i

You can add an item with no cost yet — it just shows as visibly incomplete for profitability review. Add the cost as soon as you know it.

04Categories

Organize the catalog the way your business already thinks about its work.

Categories — HVAC Filters, Water Heater Equipment, Diagnostic Fees — help you browse and maintain the catalog once it grows past a handful of items. They don't change an item's price, cost, or behavior, and customers never see them. Create a category right from the item's category field.

05Aliases and common items

Two small settings that make field search work.

Aliases are alternate search words for the same item — a "Condensate Pump" might carry condensate, drain pump, and CP20. They help staff find the existing item instead of creating a duplicate under a different name. An alias never creates a second item, price, or record.

Common item marks something the team uses often, so it surfaces first in field workflows. Keep this list short — that's where its value comes from. Use it for everyday labor lines, standard service charges, and recurring fees.

06Assemblies

A reusable bundle built around one primary offering.

When the same combination of items goes out together — a maintenance visit, a standard repair package, an equipment replacement — define it once as an assembly instead of asking staff to remember every component. Every assembly has a primary item (the main offering it represents) and a list of associated items with quantities.

Pick a price treatment that matches how you actually sell the work:

Summed Priced from the parts

Each included item contributes its own sell price. Use when a repair's labor, part, and fee are separately priced.

All-inclusive Flat package price

The primary item carries the package price; components support the work but don't add their own. Use for flat-rate maintenance or fixed-price replacement.

i

Adding an assembly to a job is a starting point, not a lock. Staff still document what's actually appropriate for that request. The same catalog item can sit in several assemblies.

07Nudges

An optional reminder shown when a related item or assembly is selected.

A nudge is a prompt, not a rule. It's attached to one trigger — a catalog item or an assembly — and offers one to three relevant suggestions. When staff pick that trigger while documenting proposed work, OpHalo shows the suggestions; staff decide whether to add them. Nothing is added automatically.

!

Use a nudge when something is often relevant but not always. Use an assembly when items always belong together. Don't nudge for every upsell — too many generic suggestions become noise and get ignored. Safety and compliance requirements need a real operational process, not a nudge.

Typical setup order

1
Create categories that match your natural service, material, and equipment groups.
2
Add your most common items first — names, units, costs, sell prices.
3
Add SKUs and aliases where they help the team find the right item.
4
Mark only truly frequent items as Common.
5
Create assemblies for repeatable multi-item jobs.
6
Add a few targeted nudges for common related work.
7
Review quarterly as supplier costs, pricing, and offerings change.

What it is not

  • Not inventory. No warehouse quantities or stock tracking.
  • Not purchasing. No supplier orders or purchase orders.
  • Not invoicing or accounting. No payments, invoices, or QuickBooks sync.
  • Not a spreadsheet import. The MVP is direct, deliberate entry in OpHalo. One currency: USD.
  • It is the foundation for consistent scope capture, work records, pricing review, and future quoting.

Common questions

Do we have to enter the whole catalog before we can use it?

No. Start with the items your team uses most — common services, service-call fees, frequent parts, standard equipment. Add more over time. A smaller accurate catalog beats a large one full of duplicates.

Can technicians see our costs and margins?

Not in the field selection workflow. Field staff pick approved items and assemblies to document work; the office keeps control of cost, sell price, and profitability review.

What if we don't know an item's cost yet?

Add it without a cost. It will show as visibly incomplete for internal review. Fill in the cost as soon as it's known.

Can an item have a cost but no standalone sell price?

Yes — useful for a small fitting included inside a flat-rate or all-inclusive assembly. Tick "This item doesn't have its own sell price" under Advanced options.

Assembly or nudge — which do we use?

Assembly when items usually belong together as a standard package. Nudge when an item is often relevant but not always appropriate — for example, a drain-line service may nudge a float-switch check, but shouldn't force it onto every job.

What happens if a tech needs something not in the catalog?

They can document a one-off off-catalog item when appropriate. It does not automatically become a Price Book item or get a price. The office can later decide whether to curate it in for future use.

Do aliases change the price or create another item?

No. An alias is only another way to find the same item. No duplicate item, no separate cost or sell price.

Can we rename or retire an item later?

Yes. Maintain the catalog as the business changes. Retire items that are no longer used rather than leaving them selectable for new work. Existing records keep their historical price/cost context.

Who should own Price Book maintenance?

Usually an Owner, Admin, office manager, or operational lead who understands both the service workflow and pricing decisions. Field feedback is valuable, but changes to cost, prices, assemblies, and nudges stay centrally maintained.

How often should we review it?

Whenever supplier costs, standard pricing, services, or common repair patterns change. A short monthly or quarterly review is usually enough once the initial setup is done.
